Ground Truth Annotation Artifact in Code-Switched Segments

The asr_segments ground truth uses a dual-form convention for code-switched words, writing both the Marathi phonetic form and the English source in parentheses — e.g., कॉफी(coffee), आय(I) एम(am) ऑलवेज(always) देअर(there). ASR systems output only one form: Whisper tends to commit to English (coffee, I am always there) while Saaras outputs the Marathi phonetic form (कॉफी, आय एम). Both are acoustically correct transcriptions, but both incur WER penalties against the dual-form GT. This makes raw cpWER numbers unreliable for code-switched segments without prior GT normalization — either stripping parentheticals to keep only the spoken Marathi form, or normalizing ASR output script before scoring. Failing to do this inflates WER artificially and obscures where ASR is genuinely failing versus where it is simply using a different but valid script convention.


GT diarization is actually slightly exceeding the length of the videos - Might fix later....Bracket Convention for Code-Switched Glosses Is Not Uniform

The dual-form convention above is written with three different bracket styles, not one. Round parens dominate (22,050 occurrences across 4,562 segments in 96 videos), but 521 glosses in 76 segments across 8 videos use square brackets — டவுட் [doubt], தேங்க்யூ [thankyou] — and 10 glosses in a single video (Iare1Emeueg) use curly braces — ट्राई {try}, लैपटॉप {laptop}. Critically, 16 segments across 8 videos mix more than one style within the same segment, so the style cannot be selected per-video. A normalizer written against round parens alone silently leaves square- and curly-bracket glosses in the reference, inflating WER exactly where code-switching is densest.

Three further traps in the same convention: 29 segments have unbalanced or doubled parens (((Stardom)), ଫାସିଲିଟିଜ((facilities)) which a naive re.sub(r'\([^)]*\)','') corrupts rather than cleans; 1,831 glosses are followed by a re-attached native case suffix (चॅनल(channel)-वर, ସେକ୍ସନରେ(section)-ରେ) that leaves a dangling hyphen-suffix token once the gloss is stripped; and 1,300 glosses are numerals (एक(1), दीड(1.5)) where the choice of which form to keep changes the reference token count.


Square Brackets in Transcript Text Break Naive Timestamp Parsing

Because 76 asr_segments entries contain literal square brackets as glosses, the [start-end] timestamp prefix cannot be found with a global regex scan over the raw field — re.findall(r'\[([\d.]+)-([\d.]+)\]') and any re.split on brackets will pick up gloss text as segment boundaries. The field must be split on the | separator first, then the timestamp regex anchored at the start of each piece. Done that way, all 9,942 segments parse with zero failures, and diarization_segments[n] and asr_segments[n] are confirmed to carry byte-identical timestamps for every single segment, so joining speaker to text by index and by time are exactly equivalent.


Ground Truth Extends Past the Requested Clip Window

85 of 100 rows have ground-truth segments ending after end_sec — median +1.43 s, max +6.41 s (Iqhnt8ENm3U) — and the overrun is uncorrelated with clip length (r = -0.11), so it is a systematic annotation-window offset rather than timing drift. Three rows go the other way with long unannotated tails: Cku_X_SL7qU ends 90.3 s before the clip end, OxYCBQKZ3iY 12.4 s, CO_8ppdzq9U 6.1 s. Since the brief mandates using only [start_sec, end_sec], the audio cannot be extended to cover the annotation; the reference must instead be cropped to a UEM of [0, end_sec - start_sec]. Failing to crop scores hypothesis silence against reference speech that was never in the audio, inflating missed-speech error on 85% of the corpus.

Two ground-truth segments are outright malformed and must be dropped rather than clamped: QuA_B6IZ6Ls segment 93 is Speaker C [939.09-155.91] (end before start by 783 s; neighbours sit at 931.5 and 947.0, so ~945.9 was likely intended), and 6ZeRgvDHwcI segment 0 is Speaker A [1.61-1.61] (zero duration). Both carry only non-speech tags.


Overlap Is Structural, and Pairwise Summation Overstates It

7.16% of total corpus time has two or more distinct speakers active, measured by a sweep line over elementary intervals. Summing pairwise overlaps instead gives 8.49%, because that double-counts three-way overlap — worth noting since the brief forbids ignoring overlap, so the number reported must be the union-based one. Only 9 of 100 clips contain no overlap at all; QuA_B6IZ6Ls is 35% overlapped and seven more clips exceed 19%. Alongside this, 20% of reference segments are shorter than 0.5 s (1,953 of 9,940) and 35% are under 1 s — these are backchannels that most diarizers either miss entirely or, worse, emit as spurious speaker turns. Reference turns are also utterance-level rather than turn-level: 22.4% of adjacent segment pairs share a speaker label, so a hypothesis that merges consecutive same-speaker utterances is not wrong and must be compared against a merged view of the reference too.


Video Availability Must Be Judged by a Real Fetch, Not an oEmbed Probe

Of the 99 unique video IDs, YouTube's oEmbed endpoint reports two as unreachable, but only one actually is. GUVrL5ltiP4 is genuinely deleted (yt-dlp: "Video unavailable"). 2HGP34TNvjg returns HTTP 401 from oEmbed, which looks identical to a private video but only means embedding is disabled — yt-dlp downloads it without trouble. The practical ceiling for extraction is therefore 99/100 rows, not 98. Note also that video_id is not a unique key: RL2fhIEEbZg appears in two rows with the disjoint windows 0-65 and 66-1034, so any per-video cache keyed on video_id alone silently drops one of the two clips.


Which yt-dlp Player Client Works Is Unstable and Unrelated to Format Quality

The client exposing the best formats is frequently not one that can actually download. yt-dlp's default client rotation advertises audio-only DASH formats (itags 139/140/251, opus at ~109 kbps) but those media URLs answer 403 Forbidden without a PO token; forcing player_client=android exposes only itag 18 (muxed 360p AVC + AAC) yet downloads reliably. Which rung works varies by network, video and yt-dlp version, so the client has to be selected by trial at run time rather than hard-coded — and a 403 on the media URL should be classified as a retryable client problem, not a generic network error. Pinning a client "to be safe" is actively harmful: it suppresses the audio-only formats and forces a muxed video download for audio-only work.

On accuracy, ffmpeg input seeking (-ss before -i) against a resolved HTTP media URL was verified byte-identical to downloading the full track and trimming locally, on multiple clips. The range fetch is therefore sample-exact, not merely approximate, and is safe to use as the primary path despite ground-truth timestamps being relative to start_sec.


Python's \w Silently Destroys Indic Text in WER Normalization

The idiomatic way to strip punctuation before scoring — re.sub(r'[^\w\s]', ' ', text) — is actively wrong for every Indic script in this corpus. Python's \w is Unicode-aware but keys off str.isalnum(), and combining marks are not alphanumeric: vowel signs (U+093E DEVANAGARI VOWEL SIGN AA and its 40-odd siblings) and viramas (U+094D, U+0BCD, U+0C4D, ...) are Unicode category Mn, so the expression deletes them. नमस्कार becomes नमस क र, and a three-token phrase becomes seven. Measured on this reference it inflates the token count from 124,207 to 126,291 and shreds the exact characters that distinguish one word from another, so WER computed that way is meaningless while looking perfectly plausible. The correct filter tests unicodedata.category(ch)[0] in 'PS', which removes real punctuation — including the danda U+0964, 1,706 occurrences — while leaving category M marks intact.

Related normalization requirements measured on the same text: 154 segments are not in NFC (Bengali ড় U+09DC and য় U+09DF carry composition exclusions, so NFC decomposes them to base+nukta), and without NFC an identical-looking reference and hypothesis compare unequal. 193 ZWNJ (U+200C) and 1 ZWJ occurrences need removing. Digits are essentially all ASCII (2,245) with exactly one Devanagari and one Telugu digit, so digit-system unification is a non-issue here.


Gloss Annotation Is a Sixth of the Reference Token Count

Stripping the dual-form code-switch glosses removes 24,914 of 149,121 reference tokens — 16.7%. Scored raw, that is a cpWER floor of roughly one in six words that has nothing to do with any ASR system, since no system emits both forms of a word. After stripping, the reference is 99.975% native script: only 31 Latin tokens remain in 124,207. That is a useful sanity signal in itself — it confirms the parenthetical really is annotation rather than transcribed speech, so keeping the native head word is the defensible reading of "what was spoken".

The suffix-reattachment rule needs two branches, not one. Of 1,827 occurrences, 1,442 need the suffix joined onto the head (चॅनल(channel)-वर is spoken चॅनलवर) while 385 need it dropped because the head already carries it (ஷோக்கு(show)-க்கு is spoken ஷோக்கு, and joining yields the doubled ஷோக்குக்கு). The split is close to language-aligned — Marathi/Hindi join, Tamil drops — but testing head.endswith(suffix) covers both without needing to know the language. A further 13 cases are not suffixes at all but chained glossed numerals (आठ(8)-नऊ(9), दोन(2)-तीन(3)) which must stay two words; they are separable by requiring the suffix to be followed by whitespace or end-of-string, which fails when another gloss follows.


Cropping the Reference to the Audio Window Is Safe; Dropping Non-Speech Tags Is Not

Cropping ground truth to the UEM [0, end_sec - start_sec] drops 38 segments entirely and truncates 88, costing 177.7 s of 44,193 s (0.4%). Critically it changes the speaker set on zero of 100 clips, so speaker-count accuracy is unaffected and the crop is free of side effects. Unioning each speaker's self-overlapping intervals merges 340 pairs; without it the same second of one speaker is counted twice in the DER denominator.

By contrast, dropping the 404 tag-only segments (<noise>, <laughter>, <unintelligible>, 294 s, 0.67% of corpus) erases Speaker D entirely from 0p6cktLGIfY__12_930, changing that clip's reference speaker count from 6 to 5. They are annotated speaker activity and belong in the diarization reference even though they carry no words for WER. Measuring cross-speaker overlap after all of this gives 7.13% of corpus time, confirming nothing quietly stopped scoring the overlap the assignment mandates.


Ground-Truth-Derived Language Hints Are a Leak, and an Easy One to Miss

The assignment states the ground truth is never an input to the pipeline, and the obvious violation — passing the true speaker count to the diarizer — is easy to remember. A subtler one is language identification: deriving a per-clip language from the dominant Unicode script of the reference transcript and feeding it to a multilingual ASR as its language code. Across nine languages that is a substantial free advantage the pipeline should have to earn from audio, and it looks innocuous because the column reads like metadata rather than ground truth. The fix that actually holds is structural rather than documentary: a frozen ClipInput type carrying only clip_id, video window, and wav path is what the pipeline stages accept, so the speaker count and the language hint are not merely discouraged but absent. Naming helps too — prefixing every ground-truth-derived column with ref_ makes provenance visible at the call site.


A Handful of Reference Speakers Are Acoustically Undetectable

Eight reference speakers across seven clips hold under two seconds of total speech, and the extreme case is a single 0.04 s segment — MmtWBUK2DKE__0_310 Speaker C says जी once, for forty milliseconds, and that is the speaker's entire existence in the reference. No clustering-based diarizer will ever emit a separate cluster for forty milliseconds of audio; the embedding window alone is longer than the segment. These speakers are therefore guaranteed misses, and because speaker-count accuracy is an exact-match metric, a clip with a 0.04 s speaker is unwinnable no matter how good the system is. Seven clips (7%) contain a speaker holding under 1% of the clip's speech. This is worth reporting as a floor on speaker-count accuracy rather than being mistaken for model weakness, and it is a strong argument for reporting mean absolute count error alongside exact-match: missing a 0.04 s speaker and hallucinating three extra ones are not the same failure.

Related: 26 speakers first appear after the midpoint of their clip, several past the 400 s mark. Online or streaming diarizers, and any approach that fixes the speaker inventory from an early window, will systematically miss these.


Overlap Is Not Mostly Backchannel, and a Fifth of It Is Long

Splitting the 4,678 cross-speaker overlap events by duration changes the picture considerably. Events under 0.5 s — the backchannel-shaped ones — are the majority by count (2,871) but only 19.1% of overlapped time. Events longer than five seconds number just 48, yet carry 21.7% of all overlap time, and the longest single overlap runs 55.6 s. Sustained two-to-five-second overlaps add another 15.1%. So roughly 37% of overlapped time is genuine sustained simultaneous speech rather than short interjections, which is the regime where a one-speaker-per-frame model cannot represent the truth at all.

Reinforcing that: 2,088 cross-speaker segments are fully nested inside another speaker's turn — an interjection wholly contained within someone else's speech. A diarizer emitting a single label per frame is structurally incapable of expressing these, so they convert directly into missed-speech error that no threshold tuning can recover.


Half of Speaker Boundaries Have No Silence to Find

Across 3,915 cross-speaker transitions the median gap is 0.250 s, but the tail matters more than the median: 17.8% of transitions have a gap of zero or less (the speakers touch or overlap), 34.6% are within 0.10 s, and 50% are within 0.25 s. Voice-activity-based segmentation has nothing acoustic to key on at these boundaries. This also explains why the conventional 0.25 s forgiveness collar is so consequential on this corpus — it would erase the region containing half of all speaker changes, which is precisely why the brief's no-collar requirement is the honest setting and why the collared variant should never be the headline. Turn churn is high too: a median of 9.2 speaker changes per minute, with the busiest clip at 34.4.


Impossible Speech Rates Reveal Misplaced Text, Not Bad Transcription

Computing tokens per second on the normalized spoken form exposes 30 segments whose rate is physically impossible. (An earlier count of 14 was wrong: it filtered to segments longer than 0.5 s, which excluded the very shortest — and therefore worst — offenders.) The worst is Cku_X_SL7qU__60_660 [507.77-507.97]: 63 tokens in 0.20 seconds, or 315 tokens/s. The transcript there is fluent, plausible Gujarati-English code-switch; it is the timestamp that is wrong, so the text belongs to a much longer span. Nine clips contain at least one such segment, and Cku_X_SL7qU alone has twelve — the same clip already flagged for ending its annotation 90 s before the clip does, so it appears to have a systematically damaged region rather than isolated typos.

QuA_B6IZ6Ls__61_1863 has a second, distinct fault: a segment spanning [108.11-1017.40], 909 seconds carrying only 57 tokens, while its neighbours are ordinary 30 s turns. That clip already contributes the dropped [939.09-155.91] segment. Both faults are end-timestamp corruption on the same clip.

Total affected duration is only 23.5 s, so DER is barely moved — but the consequence for cpWER is much larger, because the words are attributed to the wrong span and, via the speaker at that span, potentially to the wrong speaker. These segments are worth excluding or hand-correcting for ASR scoring while being harmless to leave in for diarization; the brief explicitly permits hand-labelling a subset and calling out the limitation.

For reference, legitimate rates cluster tightly by language, from a median 2.33 tokens/s for Telugu to 3.40 for Punjabi, corpus median 2.72. Those numbers are not comparable across scripts — they reflect word-segmentation convention as much as speaking speed — but within a language they make an effective outlier detector.


Repeated Text Across a Speaker Boundary Is Already Present in the Ground Truth

206 consecutive segment pairs carry identical normalized text. 138 are same-speaker, which is ordinary backchannel repetition (हं twice in a row). The other 68 are cross-speaker, and many have a negative gap, meaning the two segments overlap: 0p6cktLGIfY has Speaker C and Speaker E both saying સપના વિનાની રાત with a -0.31 s gap, and 2gGHQgj9yno has three different speakers saying જય શ્રી કૃષ્ણ within 0.04 s of each other. Some are genuine chorus (a greeting said together), others look like annotation splitting one utterance across two labels. Either way this is exactly the false-split signature the brief suggests exploiting in Step 4, and it is worth knowing that the pattern exists in the reference itself, not only in model output — a Step 4 heuristic that merges repeated text across a boundary will sometimes be "correcting" toward something the reference also does.


Two Structural Conventions Worth Relying On, and One Exception

Speaker labels follow first-appearance order in 99 of 100 clips: Speaker A is the first voice heard, then B, and so on. The single exception is 6ZeRgvDHwcI__6_100, where Speaker B is heard first — and that is the clip whose first segment is the dropped zero-duration [1.61-1.61] Speaker A entry, so the convention is probably intact in the original annotation and only broken by the corrupt segment. This means label identity carries information: comparing a system's first-emitted speaker against Speaker A is meaningful, and it gives Step 4 a cheap prior for mapping.

Separately, every ground-truth timestamp lands on a 10 ms grid (19,663 of 19,880 values have exactly two decimal places). Reporting metrics to more than about three significant figures implies precision the annotation does not have.

BRIEF NOTES NOT WORTH A SECTION OF THEIR OWN

Duration distribution is bimodal, not continuous: 38 clips are under 120 s and 27 exceed 600 s,
with almost nothing in between. Any per-clip average is therefore dominated by whichever mode it
happens to weight, which is a second reason corpus metrics must pool seconds rather than average
per-clip rates.

Total clip audio is 12.28 h out of 29.3 h of source video, so a windowed fetch moves roughly 58%
fewer bytes than downloading whole videos — though only if the player client exposes audio-only
formats, which on a bot-gated network it may not.

The corpus is 26 clips at 2 speakers, 29 at 3, 28 at 4, and only 17 at 5 or more. Models capped at
4 speakers (NVIDIA Sortformer, for one) would degrade on 17% of clips, which is why the Step 2
roster avoided them.

Segment count per clip ranges from 5 to 556. The 556-segment clip alone contributes 5.6% of all

     The two numerators differ and must not be mixed. 1362 s is DURATION where
     any speaker is predicted; 1421 s is that duration weighted by how many
     speakers are predicted, which is what DER counts and can exceed the
     available duration when two predicted speakers overlap in a silent region.
     An earlier note quoted 51% from 1421/2769, a speaker-weighted numerator
     over a duration denominator. The correct coverage figure is 49%; the
     correct DER contribution is 3.19 points. The conclusion is unchanged
     because the two nearly coincide -- most silent-region false alarms involve
     a single predicted speaker -- but the ratio was not well formed.

     Given [21], much of this is likely unannotated speech rather than
     hallucination, which would make it a measurement floor rather than a
     tuning target. That is a listening question, not a computation, and it is
     unresolved.

[24] Where the model is genuinely weak, stated so it cannot be waved away.

     (a) MID-TURN CONFUSION, 6.45 points, 24% of all error, 2,585 regions.
         This is speech that both the reference and the model agree is speech,
         attributed to the wrong speaker, more than 0.5 s away from any
         reference speaker change. A ground-truth GAP produces false alarm; an
         over-long ground-truth LABEL produces miss. Neither produces
         confusion. Only a wrong speaker IDENTITY in the reference could, and
         that is a different defect from the ones found so far. This is the
         embedding failing to separate two voices, and it is the single
         largest addressable component of the benchmark.

         For contrast, confusion within 0.5 s of a speaker change is 3.08
         points across 5,160 regions -- twice the region count for half the
         time, i.e. exactly the sliver profile of boundary jitter. The two are
         different failures and should not be reported as one number.

         The corpus has 52.5 minutes of overlapped speech; community-1 and
         pyannote-3.1 each predict 36.9 minutes, 70% of it. Where the reference
         says two people are talking the model reports one, and the second is
         charged as miss. Ground truth cannot inflate this: annotating overlap
         is a positive assertion that two people speak, and an annotator who
         is sloppy about overlap will under-mark it, which would make the true
         figure worse rather than better.

     explanation. The conclusion "the main weakness is ground-truth

     A system at 0.35 is 0.23 above the achievable floor, not 0.35 of avoidable

[27] Two metric definitions were wrong on the first pass; both were caught by
     tests rather than review, and both would have changed conclusions.

     * WDER was written as "correct words with the wrong speaker, over correct
       words". The definition (El Shafey, Soltau & Shafran, Interspeech 2019)
       is (S_IS + C_IS) / (S + C): substitutions count on BOTH sides, because a
       misrecognised word still carries an attribution that can be wrong.
       Insertions and deletions are excluded from both, having no speaker pair
       to disagree about.

     * DI-cpWER was implemented by concatenating each speaker's words and
       joining the groups. Grouping by speaker is exactly what makes a metric
       diarization-DEPENDENT, so the "invariant" figure still moved with
       attribution and cpWER - DI-cpWER measured nothing. It now takes flat
       word streams with no speaker structure at all. Caught because a perfect
       ASR plus a perfect diarizer scored 0.08 where it had to score 0.

     Also settled: cpWER's assignment does NOT need a permutation search, and
     Hungarian is not an approximation of it. A (reference speaker, hypothesis
     speaker) pair's error count does not depend on which other pairs are
     chosen, so the total is a sum of independent cells and minimising it IS
     linear assignment. Verified against brute force on 396 (clip, corruption)
     cases spanning 2 to 40,320 permutations: zero disagreements.

     (a) TOTAL SPEECH. DiariZen predicts 96% of the reference's union speech

           model            onset med   offset med   inward both   ref wider
           diarizen-large     +0.023       -0.137        39%          72%
           community-1        -0.010       -0.139        31%          58%
           pyannote-3.1       -0.022       -0.101        29%          55%
           reverb-v2          -6.577       +5.983         7%          12%

         15.5 s mean turn against a 2.6 s one means.

         aligned (+0.023 s); offsets end 137 ms early. And community-1 shows
         -0.139 and pyannote-3.1 -0.101 -- the same trailing bias. That makes it
         a property of the REFERENCE, not of DiariZen: annotations run ~100-140
         ms past where every model says speech stopped.

     (c) DILATION, the direct intervention. Padding turns by 0.1 s:

           model            miss before -> after   FA added   recovered/FA
           diarizen-large     0.1143 -> 0.0805      +0.0331       1.02
           pyannote-3.1       0.1079 -> 0.0820      +0.0258       1.00
           community-1        0.1079 -> 0.0816      +0.0310       0.85
           reverb-v2          0.0719 -> 0.0689      +0.0092       0.33

         DiariZen's miss is by far the most boundary-adjacent: 100 ms recovers
         30% of it, against Reverb's 4%. So the hypothesis is right.

     Why it does not pay. False alarm rises about 1:1 with the miss recovered,
     so DER barely moves. Padding is blind -- it cannot tell a turn whose
     reference is loose from one whose neighbouring silence is real, so it lands
     on genuine non-speech about as often as on annotator slack.

     Exploiting the asymmetry helps, and confirms the diagnosis: extending only
     the trailing edge beats symmetric padding roughly twofold, because
     symmetric spends half its budget on the leading edge where the measured
     bias is ~0.

           diarizen-large   sym 0.10s   DER -0.0016
                            after 0.10s DER -0.0031   <- best
           community-1      sym 0.10s   DER +0.0026   (symmetric actively hurts)
                            after 0.10s DER -0.0009

     -0.0031 DER, 1.2% relative. Real, reproducible, and far too small to be an
     improvement worth reporting as one.

     What would exploit it. The padding needs a signal for WHERE to extend
     rather than extending everywhere. ASR word timings are exactly that: carry
     a turn's trailing edge forward only as far as recognised words continue,
     and stop at the first real silence. That converts a blind 100 ms guess into
     evidence, and Step 3 produces the evidence as a byproduct. Worth trying in
     Step 4 -- but sized honestly against the ~0.003 DER that blind padding
     already reaches, since the ceiling on this whole effect is small.

[33] 27 of 99 clips have ground truth displaced in time by up to 4.6 seconds.
     This is the largest single distortion in the benchmark, and correcting it
     REVERSES the model ranking.

     Hypothesis under test, from watching the source videos: "GT timestamps on
     several clips start 1-2 s later than the speech."

     Method. A global time shift needs no speaker mapping, so the test runs on
     raw speech activity: rasterise reference and hypothesis at 10 ms, cross-
     correlate, take the lag maximising agreement (evaluation.estimate_reference_lag).
     A positive lag means the reference is LATER than the speech the models
     heard. The evidence is cross-model AGREEMENT -- four diarizers with
     different segmenters have no reason to be wrong in the same direction by
     the same amount on the same clip.

     Result: 27 clips where all four agree on a lag beyond 0.5 s, spread across
     models never exceeding 0.54 s, lags from +0.54 s to +4.64 s.

     Confirmation by re-scoring. Undoing the shift on those 27 clips:

       model            DER as measured -> aligned    relative
       diarizen-large        0.2625  ->  0.1919        -26.9%
       community-1           0.2634  ->  0.2039        -22.6%
       pyannote-3.1          0.2637  ->  0.2059        -21.9%
       reverb-v2             0.2521  ->  0.2360         -6.4%

     Individual clips move from nonsense to normal: 2iMXYxBTwbM__339_401 goes
     0.848 -> 0.103 for DiariZen, 7c9ePKgqams__299_869 0.370 -> 0.073.

     IT IS NOT OUR EXTRACTION. Re-cutting a flagged clip with accurate seeking
     (-ss AFTER -i, sample-accurate) gives byte-identical alignment to the
     original (-ss BEFORE -i): lag +3.91 s either way, matching the +3.92 s the
     diarizers agreed on. The published WAV is a correct cut of
     [start_sec, end_sec].

     The mechanism follows from the arithmetic. If the annotator's window began
     D seconds BEFORE start_sec, their timestamps run D larger than ours. D
     cannot be negative when start_sec is 0 -- and of the 26 clips with
     start_sec == 0, exactly ZERO are affected (median lag -0.010 s, max
     0.01 s), against 27 of the 73 that need a seek (median +1.935 s). Nothing
     else correlates: corr(lag, start_sec) is only +0.333, so it is not a fixed
     offset or a scaling, and no script or speaker count predicts it.

     WHY THIS MATTERS MOST. The ranking is an artifact of the misalignment.
     reverb-v2 looked best at 0.2521 only because its turns are long and loose
     (15.5 s mean) and therefore robust to a shifted reference; it gains least
     from correction. DiariZen, the most precise model, was punished hardest.
     Aligned, the order inverts completely:

       diarizen-large 0.1919 < community-1 0.2039 < pyannote-3.1 0.2059 < reverb-v2 0.2360

     obs [29]-[31] were computed on the uncorrected reference and their
     conclusions about which model owns which component must be re-derived
     before the hybrid in [31] is built on them.

     HOW IT IS REPORTED. The brief fixes the scoring protocol, so the headline
     numbers stay as measured against the ground truth as given. The aligned
     figures are a named diagnostic alongside them. The brief also says: "If for
     any reason the ground-truth labels look incorrect, hand-label a subset and
     call out the limitation" -- this is that call-out, with the subset
     identified automatically and confirmed by eye on the source videos.

[34] Step 4: majority-vote fusion of three diarizations. +3.1% on the brief's
     protocol, and the only system that is never near-worst on either half.

     Method: DOVER-Lap (Raj et al., SLT 2021). Map each system's speaker labels
     onto a growing centroid by Hungarian on frame overlap -- the same
     assignment problem cpWER solves, exact for the same reason -- then vote per
     10 ms frame, thresholding each speaker INDEPENDENTLY so overlap survives.
     No ground truth is used; the only fitted quantity is the vote threshold.

     Corpus, 99 clips:

       system            DER    miss      FA    conf     JER  spk acc  aligned
       FUSION         0.2442  0.1138  0.0498  0.0806  0.3608   79.8%   0.1850
       reverb-v2      0.2521  0.0719  0.0681  0.1121  0.3834   60.6%   0.2360
       diarizen-large 0.2625  0.1143  0.0640  0.0842  0.3660   72.7%   0.1919
       community-1    0.2634  0.1079  0.0602  0.0954  0.3768   78.8%   0.2039
       pyannote-3.1   0.2637  0.1079  0.0602  0.0957  0.3785   71.7%   0.2059

     Best on DER, false alarm, confusion, JER, speaker-count accuracy and
     aligned DER; worse only on miss, where it sits with the pyannote pair
     rather than with reverb-v2. Voting cannot recover speech that a majority
     never found, which is the mechanism.

     Two design choices, both measured rather than assumed:

     * EQUAL WEIGHTS, not DOVER-Lap's 1/rank. Rank weighting gives the leader 1
       and the runner-up 0.5, so with two systems the leader alone clears any
       threshold below 0.67 and the fusion silently degenerates into "use the
       best system" -- it returned reverb-v2's dev score to four decimal places.
       Equal weights beat it 0.2105 to 0.2223 on dev.

     * THREE SYSTEMS, dropping pyannote-3.1. It and community-1 differ by 0.44 s
       of miss+FA over 12.4 h ([18]), so including both double-weights one
       segmentation and lets a single lineage carry a majority. Dropping it
       improved dev from 0.2181 to 0.2105.

     Chosen on dev (50 clips), test (49) scored once: 0.2481 against the best
     single system's 0.2600, +4.6%.

     THE HONEST CAVEAT, which is also the most useful finding here. Dev and test
     disagree violently about which SINGLE model is best -- aligned, reverb-v2
     runs 0.2146 on dev and 0.2524 on test while diarizen-large runs 0.2326 and
     0.1606. Every single system ranks 1st on one half and 4th or 5th on the
     other. With ~50 clips per half and a duration-pooled DER dominated by a
     handful of long clips, a 0.02-0.04 DER gap is inside split noise, and the
     "best model" claim in [29]/[33] should be read with that in mind.

     The fusion is the only entry that ranks 1st or 2nd on BOTH halves under
     BOTH protocols. Its value here is less that it is 3% better on average and
     more that it removes the variance -- picking any single model amounts to
     betting on which half of the corpus you drew.

       reference : ನಾನು ಒಂದು ವರ್ಷದಿಂದ ಆಪ್ ಯೂಸ್ ಮಾಡ್ತಾ ಇದ್ದೀನಿ
       turbo     : I am the only way to use my work. I have learned this...

     large-v3 gets the same audio right, so this is specific to the distilled
     model: turbo keeps the encoder but cuts the decoder from 32 layers to 4,
     and language identification degraded with it. The speed came at a cost that
     is not visible in a WER benchmark for English.

     Fix, implemented: split the two jobs. large-v3 identifies the language --
     an encoder-only pass over ~30 s, cheap -- and turbo does the decoding,
     which is where its speed actually lives. Detection uses 4 segments rather
     than 1, since a code-switched clip can open on an English phrase and
     committing the whole clip from the first window is how "en" wins.

     This is NOT a ground-truth leak. The language comes from a model listening
     to the audio; ref_lang_script is derived from the reference transcript and
     remains unavailable to every pipeline stage.

     The wider lesson for the writeup: a model swap justified on throughput was
     validated on throughput alone. Language identification is upstream of
     everything else in a multilingual pipeline, and it fails silently -- the
     output looks like fluent text, just in the wrong language.

[38] The best diarization is not the best ASR front-end, and the reason is that
     DER and cpWER punish false alarm completely differently.

     Saaras was run over reverb-v2's turns rather than the fusion's, which looks
     wrong: the fusion has the better DER (0.2442 against 0.2521) and wins on
     five of six diarization metrics. Measured as ASR segmentation sources:

       diarization      merged segs  median s   <1s   ref speech never covered
       reverb-v2              2,650      7.66s   22%    1 min  (0.2%)
       FUSION                 6,312      2.50s   29%   21 min  (3.1%)
       community-1           10,279      1.13s   48%   20 min  (3.0%)
       diarizen-large        11,013      1.48s   41%   24 min  (3.5%)

     The last column decides it. Reference speech that no diarized turn covers
     is never sent to the recogniser, so it cannot be transcribed at any
     quality -- it is a guaranteed cpWER deletion. reverb-v2 leaves 1 minute of
     the corpus uncovered; the fusion leaves 21.

     The asymmetry is the point. In DER, a second of false alarm and a second of
     miss cost the same. In per-segment ASR they do not: false alarm means
     transcribing a little non-speech, which yields a few spurious words and
     costs some insertions, while miss means audio the recogniser never sees,
     which costs a full deletion and cannot be recovered downstream. A
     diarization tuned to minimise DER is therefore NOT tuned for this job.

     reverb-v2 is the model DER likes least among the strong ones -- worst
     confusion, worst speaker-count accuracy, last place once the reference is
     aligned -- and the best available ASR front-end, because it over-generates
     speech. Its 15.5 s turns and 106% coverage of reference speech are exactly
     the properties that make it look mediocre on DER.

     Secondary but real: 2,650 segments against 6,312 is 2.4x fewer API calls,
     and a 7.66 s median against 2.50 s gives the recogniser more context per
     request.

     THAT CONCLUSION IS WRONG, and [39] retracts it. Coverage is only half the
     trade, and the half I measured. See below.

[39] Retracts [38]. reverb-v2 is the WORST ASR front-end, not the best --
     coverage is real but attribution dominates it, and I measured only one side.

     [38] argued reverb-v2 was the right ASR segmentation source because it
     leaves almost no reference speech uncovered, and uncovered speech is an
     unrecoverable cpWER deletion. That is true and it is not the whole trade.
     Its 15.5 s turns routinely span several reference speakers, and per-segment
     ASR gives every word in a turn ONE label, so every word of the minority
     speaker in an impure turn is misattributed. In cpWER a misattributed word
     costs twice a missing one -- a deletion from the speaker who said it and an
     insertion into the one credited.

       diarization    uncovered ref speech    impure turns   ref speech in them
       reverb-v2            1 min (0.1%)          74%            131 min
       FUSION              16 min (2.3%)          63%            107 min
       community-1         15 min (2.2%)          48%            112 min
       diarizen-large      18 min (2.6%)          54%            106 min

     Settled by holding the recogniser perfect: feed the reference transcript
     back as if recognised, attribute it through each diarization, and score.
     The recogniser drops out and what remains is the cpWER floor that
     diarization imposes, with coverage and misattribution weighted the way
     cpWER actually weights them rather than by my arithmetic. Costs nothing --
     no audio, no API calls.

       ALIGNED reference    cpWER   DI-cpWER   attribution    WDER
       diarizen-large      0.1911     0.0910       0.1002   0.0942
       FUSION              0.2070     0.0923       0.1147   0.0958
       community-1         0.2272     0.0905       0.1366   0.1126
       reverb-v2           0.2825     0.0822       0.2003   0.1357

     reverb-v2 has the BEST DI-cpWER, which is coverage and confirms [38]'s
     observation exactly -- and the WORST cpWER, because its attribution cost is
     nearly double the fusion's. The advantage I identified is real and is
     outweighed roughly two to one by the cost I did not measure.

     Consequence: the Saaras sweep ran over the worst available front-end. Its
     cpWER of 0.3957 carries a 0.2825 floor from reverb-v2 alone; the same
     transcripts attributed through diarizen-large would face a 0.1911 floor.
     Up to ~0.09 cpWER is recoverable by re-cutting on a different diarization,
     which is far larger than anything else still on the table.

     Cost of re-running: diarizen-large gives 11,013 merged segments against
     reverb-v2's 2,650, so 4.2x the API calls; the FUSION gives 6,312, so 2.4x.
     The fusion is within 0.016 cpWER of diarizen-large's floor for 43% fewer
     calls, and it is already the Step 4 system, so using it keeps one story
     rather than two.

     The methodological lesson, which is the reason this is written down: [38]
     reasoned from one measured quantity to a confident recommendation without
     measuring the quantity that opposed it. The oracle-floor experiment that
     settles it takes seconds and needs no audio. It should have come first.

[42] Retracts the mechanism in [37]. Whisper's deletions are NOT truncation,
     and repetition collapse is real but far too rare to explain them.

     Measured over all 99 whisper-large-v3-turbo outputs:

       transcripts spanning <50% of their clip     0 / 99
       rep5 > 50% (single 5-gram dominates)        1 / 99
       rep5 > 20%                                  3 / 99
       median rep5                                 3.0%

     rep5 is the share of output tokens covered by occurrences of its most
     frequent 5-gram, counted by POSITION -- consecutive repeats of one token
     produce overlapping n-grams, and counting occurrences x n gives figures
     above 100%, which is how the first version of this metric reported 391%.

     Not one transcript stops early. Every one emits words across the whole
     clip. So "greedy decoding truncates" -- the headline of [37] -- is wrong:
     the deficit is word DENSITY, roughly half as many words spread over the
     full duration, not early termination. The A/B in [37] showing beam size
     changing nothing was pointing at this all along and I read it as a null
     result rather than as evidence against the mechanism.

     The specific clip [37] cites is not a collapse case at all.
     0AEEA8NyVwY__11_609: 896 words, transcript spans 100% of the 598 s, top
     5-gram occurs ONCE, rep5 0.6%. The claim that it "transcribes 47 s
     correctly then repeats a five-word phrase for the remaining 550 s" is
     false. What is actually wrong with that clip is mis-recognition -- garbled
     Devanagari, Marathi content detected as hi, one stray CJK character.

     Collapse does exist. Kdi-ECuOaKg__2_78 reaches rep5 79.8% (272 words for
     76 s), and 83gP2vLH7UY__255_2005 produces 273 words for 1750 s of audio at
     rep5 32.6%. Three clips out of 99 cannot move a corpus ratio from 1.0 to
     0.75.

     What remains unexplained is therefore the density itself, and [41] gives
     the likeliest reason: on 92 of 99 clips the output is English translation,
     and English is more compact than the code-switched Indic source. Against
     that, the 7 in-script clips had ratio 0.74 -- the same as the 92 -- which
     the compression story does not predict. Seven clips is thin, and this is
     the open question the configuration probe is built to close.

[44] One bad segment discarded an entire clip, and it cost the two largest.
     The Saaras fusion sweep reported 97/99. The by-script table summed to 97
     with Devanagari at 24 (of 25) and Gurmukhi at 6 (of 7), and the reference
     word gap was 123,896 - 113,918 = 9,978, which identifies the two exactly:

       8o7jKmq6HsM__30_1770   Devanagari  4,454 words  1740 s  113 segments
       OxYCBQKZ3iY__0_1822    Gurmukhi    5,524 words  1822 s  476 segments

     The two longest clips in the corpus, and the second has more segments than
     any other. That is the mechanism, not a coincidence: transcribe_segments
     fans segments out with pool.map, which RE-RAISES the first exception, so a
     single non-retryable 400 anywhere in a clip propagated to run_segmented and
     the whole transcript was dropped. 476 segments means 476 chances to hit one.
     The failure rate per segment need not be high for this to be near-certain
     on the longest clips, which is why exactly the two largest were lost.

     Two things made it hard to see. The failure was logged and counted but
     never persisted, so it lived only in the kernel scrollback and vanished
     with the session; and the only surviving symptom was a clip count of 97,
     which reads as "still running" rather than "failed".

     Fixed three ways. A failing segment is now caught inside the worker and
     returns an empty row marked error:<Type>, so the clip completes with its
     other segments. meta carries n_failed_segments and the clip logs
     "transcript is INCOMPLETE", so a partial result cannot pass for a whole
     one. Clip-level failures append to logs/step3_failures.jsonl, matching the
     step1/step2 convention that already existed and that step 3 lacked.

     Verified by injecting a _Fatal into one segment of a two-segment clip: both
     rows return, one carries text, meta reports n_failed_segments 1. Before the
     fix that clip raised and was discarded.

     Worth noting the metric consequence. Those 9,978 words are 8% of the corpus
     and both clips are long, so the 97-clip figures are not a random subsample
     of the 99-clip ones -- they systematically exclude the hardest material.

     with lower deletions (19.6% vs 33.0%) and less repetition (3.9% vs 6.8%).
     Published Indic benchmarks usually favour RNNT, so this inverts the
     expectation. INFERRED, not established: RNNT looks more prone to early
     termination on short diarized segments than on the full utterances it was
     trained on -- consistent with its much higher deletion rate.

     Do NOT quote IndicConformer's WDER 0.0332 as better attribution than
     Saaras's 0.0641. Both run per-segment on the SAME fusion turns, so
     attribution is identical by construction. WDER's denominator counts only
     aligned words (S + C), and IndicConformer emits ratio 0.82 against 0.97, so
     fewer words align and the denominator shrinks. Coverage artefact.

[51] The Gemini refinement stage is a null result, and the interesting part is
     the failure the guards did NOT catch.

     10 GT-blind pilot clips (dev only), gemini-3.5-flash, temperature 0:

       system                    ratio      WER    cpWER     WDER   ins
       baseline                 0.9734   0.2357   0.2498   0.0346   223
       arm A text correction    0.9722   0.2369   0.2493   0.0334   223
       arm B + overlap cleanup  0.9700   0.2371   0.2498   0.0335   217

     Arm A mean delta -0.0014, 95% CI [-0.0054, +0.0014]. Arm B +0.0013, CI
     [-0.0008, +0.0038]. Both CIs include zero, both win 2 of 10 clips. Every
     pre-registered abandon criterion fired: CI includes zero, win rate 20%
     against a 60% bar, insertions did not fall, revert rate 29% against 20%.

     THE ARCHITECTURE WORKED. All 20 refined payloads have a segmentation
     byte-identical to source -- same segmentation_key, speakers, timestamps,
     order, count. Returning only {id, text} made changing a timestamp or a
     speaker structurally impossible rather than merely forbidden, and the
     pre-write assertion never fired once. The model was also appropriately
     conservative: 24 edits across 402 segments, and every zero-overlap control
     clip came back untouched.

     THE GUARD WAS THE WRONG SHAPE. 7 of 24 applied edits (29%) introduced a
     script that was absent from the original, and all 7 passed the R1
     dominant-script rail because the dominant script did not change:

       Telugu   పార్టీ        -> パーティー     (Japanese katakana)
       Bengali  এটাও         -> 것도          (Korean hangul)
       Marathi  हे           -> হে           (Bengali)
       + Thai, + Devanagari into Bengali

     The model is substituting a SINGLE token with a phonetically similar word
     in an unrelated script. One corrupted token in forty leaves the dominant
     script intact, so a dominant-script test cannot see it. That also explains
     why WER got slightly WORSE (0.2357 -> 0.2369) while cpWER barely moved:
     sparse token-level damage, never an improvement.

     The right rail is per-token -- reject any output token whose script is
     absent from the input segment. Two lines, and it would have caught all 7.
     Deliberately NOT applied: the prompt and guards were frozen before the run,
     and changing them after seeing the result would invalidate the pilot. It is
     recorded as the first thing to change if this is ever revisited.

     Why the null was likely, stated in advance and borne out: [50] had already
     taken the prize. The provenance fix moved cpWER 0.3381 -> 0.3181 and cut
     insertions 41%, and the free deterministic dedup rule had already reversed
     sign on the corrected baseline. Arm B matching baseline is the PREDICTED
     outcome. And arm A aims at word error that a text-only refiner cannot hear:
     Saaras's residual errors here are code-switched surface forms and ambiguous
     short turns, not linguistic implausibilities, and the model correctly
     declined to touch them.

     Operational note: the free tier caps GenerateRequestsPerDayPerProjectPerModel
     at 2/day for gemini-3.7-flash and ~20 for others, which forced whole-clip
     windows (10 calls/arm) instead of the designed 12-segment cores (37/arm).
     At 402 segments and 24 edits the pilot cannot resolve an effect below about
     0.005 cpWER, which is itself a reason not to read anything into the sign.

[52] Source separation over overlapped regions HURTS. Fourth overlap idea
     measured and rejected.

     100 GT-overlap runs from the 10 clips with the most overlapped words
     (3,851 overlapped reference words, 862s), each run passed through three
     conditions and transcribed by Saaras v3:

       A  mixture @16k (baseline)              52.6% of GT words recovered
       B  mixture 16k -> 8k -> 16k (control)   52.0%   bandwidth penalty -0.6%
       C  ConvTasNet 2 streams (Libri2Mix)     50.0%   separation effect -2.0%

     Pre-registered gate was >=65%. Delivered 50.0%.

     The control is what makes this conclusive. torchaudio's only built-in
     separator runs at 8 kHz, so the obvious objection is that the result
     measures bandwidth loss rather than separation quality. It does not: the
     round-trip through 8 kHz costs 0.6 points, while separation costs a further
     2.0. Saaras is nearly indifferent to the 8 kHz band on this material and
     clearly worse on the separated streams.

     Mechanism, inferred: ConvTasNet-Libri2Mix is trained on clean English read
     speech, exactly two speakers, fully overlapped, studio channel. This corpus
     is YouTube conversation with music and laughter, nine Indic languages, 2-8
     speakers, and PARTIAL overlap of 0.38s median. The separator emits two
     decorrelated streams (measured corr +0.04, so it is doing something) but
     the streams carry artefacts that cost more than the interference they
     remove. Saaras already recovers 52.6% from the raw mixture; that is a
     strong baseline for an out-of-domain separator to beat.

     The ceiling was real -- overlapped words are deleted at 17.9% against 4.7%
     for clean speech, and a perfect separator would be worth -0.031 WER
     (0.2787 -> 0.2479), the largest ceiling measured on this project. The
     ceiling is not the problem; the available instrument is.

     Together with [50]-[51] and the voting/MSDD/OSD audits, four independent
     overlap interventions have now been measured and none improves the system:
     lower voting threshold (+0.056 DER), MSDD-style verifier (candidates 11.5%
     precise), OSD intersection (23.6% precise, +0.0024 DER), and separation
     (-2.6 points of word recovery). Overlap remains the dominant weakness and
     no off-the-shelf component available here addresses it.

[53] The first Step 4 intervention that WORKS, and it is a lookup table.

     The reference writes code-switched English phonetically in the native
     script -- the dual-form convention behind the 20,562 parenthesised glosses.
     Measured: the reference is 99.98% Indic script (27 Latin tokens in
     123,896); the Saaras hypothesis is 3.0% Latin (3,689 tokens). Of 21,357
     substitutions, 3,481 (16.3%) are ref-Indic against hyp-Latin:

       ఐ -> i     టు -> to    ఓకే -> okay   సో -> so
       એમએલ -> ml  एंड -> and  યુ -> you     బోత్ -> both

     These are not recognition errors. Saaras heard the word and wrote it in the
     wrong script for this corpus's convention. Every one scores as a
     substitution.

     Fix: transliterate the hypothesis's Latin tokens into the clip's dominant
     script, using claude-sonnet-4-6 on Bedrock over the VOCABULARY only -- 1,651
     unique (script, token) pairs, one batch per script, cached, applied as a
     frozen lookup. No re-run of Saaras. Script comes from
     data.dominant_script on the HYPOTHESIS; the spellings come from the model's
     knowledge of the language. Ground truth is never read.

     Result, all 99 clips, 3,520 tokens converted:

                    ratio      WER    cpWER     WDER      sub     del
       before      0.9786   0.2787   0.3181   0.0628   21,357   7,914
       after       0.9786   0.2614   0.3045   0.0603   18,927   8,053
       delta      +0.0000  -0.0174  -0.0137  -0.0024   -2,430    +139

     WER -6.2% relative, cpWER -4.3%, WDER -3.8%. Substitutions fall by 2,430 --
     69% of the 3,520 conversions landed on the reference's exact spelling.

       split   mean d cpWER   95% CI            better/worse/tie
       dev        -0.0064     [-0.0104,-0.0032]   26 / 0 / 24
       test       -0.0137     [-0.0245,-0.0051]   27 / 0 / 22

     BOTH CIs exclude zero, and NOT ONE CLIP GOT WORSE on either split. That is
     by construction: the stage only rewrites tokens the recogniser emitted in
     Latin, so a wrong transliteration leaves a substitution that was already a
     substitution. The downside is bounded at zero. Deletions rise by 139, the
     only cost, where a converted token fails to align.

     Why this was invisible for so long: four overlap interventions were built
     and measured before anyone asked what the METRIC was actually penalising.
     Overlap is 27.4% of DER error and got four experiments; script convention
     was 16.3% of all substitutions and got none, because "substitution" reads
     as "misrecognised" and nobody checked. Read the error decomposition before
     choosing the intervention.

[54] A second convention mismatch, same shape as [53]: numerals. And a map of
     what the LLM can and cannot fix, from the residual error rather than guesswork.

     The corpus writes numbers as SPOKEN WORDS -- 14 digit-bearing tokens in
     123,896 reference tokens (0.01%). Saaras writes DIGITS: 892 tokens (0.74%),
     producing 836 substitutions and 56 insertions.

       ఇరవై -> 20     వంద -> 100    ਦਸ -> 10    पंद्रह -> 15    तीन -> 3

     Same fix as [53]: extract the digit vocabulary from the hypothesis, ask
     claude-sonnet-4-6 to spell each out in the clip's language, apply as a
     frozen lookup. 697 tokens converted.

     Stacked result, all 99 clips:

       stage                 ratio      WER    cpWER     WDER
       baseline             0.9786   0.2787   0.3181   0.0628
       + transliteration    0.9786   0.2614   0.3045   0.0603
       + numerals           0.9807   0.2585   0.3017   0.0602
       TOTAL               +0.0021  -0.0202  -0.0164  -0.0026

     WER -7.2% relative, cpWER -5.2%. Per clip: 72 better, 1 worse, 26 tie;
     95% CI [-0.0188, -0.0084], excludes zero. dev -0.0094 (34 better, 0 worse),
     test -0.0216 (38 better, 1 worse).

     Numerals are weaker than transliteration (-0.0028 vs -0.0136 cpWER) and
     carry the only regression: spelling a number out changes the token count,
     so a wrong reading adds insertions. Word ratio moves 0.9786 -> 0.9807.

     WHAT ELSE WAS TESTED AND REJECTED, all measured on the residual:

     - Spelling variants. 46.2% of remaining substitutions are within 2
       characters of the reference, which looks like a huge pool. But only 423
       (2.2%) vanish under orthographic folding -- nukta (ज़्यादा/ज्यादा, 311)
       and chandrabindu/anusvara (हूँ/हूं, 112), worth 0.34% of tokens, and that
       is a SCORING normalisation applied to both sides rather than a system
       improvement. The rest are genuinely different words: તે/એ is "that" vs
       "this", हैं/है is a plural marker. An LLM correcting these would have to
       guess the annotator's choice, which is exactly where [51] introduced
       cross-script corruption.
     - Names. Not a distinct failure class -- no proper noun appears in the top
       near-miss pairs, and deletions are dominated by fillers, not names.
     - Deduplication. Already measured in [50]: it helped before the provenance
       fix and HURTS after it (-0.0064 -> +0.0027 cpWER).
     - Filler recovery. The most-deleted reference tokens are backchannels the
       recogniser drops: अ(284), हं(102), ఉమ్మ్(85), ହୁଁ(83), હા(60). An LLM
       cannot restore them without inventing speech.

     The generalisable lesson: the wins came from CONVENTION mismatches, where
     the reference and the recogniser disagree about how to WRITE a correctly
     heard word, and where a general model supplies the convention without ever
     seeing the reference. The failures were all attempts to fix RECOGNITION,
     which a text-only model cannot do.
